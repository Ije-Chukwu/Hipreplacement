What Is Hip Replacement?

Hip replacement (arthroplasty) is a surgical procedure in which a damaged or arthritic hip joint is removed and replaced with an artificial implant. The surgery relieves pain, restores mobility, and improves quality of life for hundreds of thousands of patients every year.

Most people who need hip replacement have severe arthritis that limits everyday activities such as walking, bending, or climbing stairs, even after other treatments have been tried.

450K+ procedures per year in the US
95%+ patient satisfaction rate
20 yrs average implant longevity

Types of Hip Replacement

There are several surgical approaches. Your surgeon will recommend the best option based on your anatomy, activity level, and overall health.

Total Hip Replacement

The most common procedure. Both the femoral head (ball) and the acetabulum (socket) are replaced with prosthetic components. It is highly effective for osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and post-traumatic arthritis.

  • Typical surgery time: 1 – 2 hours
  • Hospital stay: 1 – 3 days
  • Walking with assistance: within 24 hours
  • Full recovery: 6 – 12 weeks

Partial Hip Replacement (Hemiarthroplasty)

Only the femoral head is replaced, leaving the natural socket intact. Most often performed after a hip fracture in older adults where the socket cartilage is still healthy.

  • Shorter operating time than total replacement
  • Suitable when socket is undamaged
  • Lower risk of dislocation in some cases
  • Commonly performed in patients over 70

Hip Resurfacing

The femoral head is reshaped and capped with a smooth metal covering rather than being removed entirely. Often chosen for younger, active patients because more bone is preserved, potentially making future revision surgery easier.

  • Best for younger, active patients
  • More bone preserved than total replacement
  • Lower dislocation risk
  • Metal-on-metal bearing surface

Recovery Timeline

Recovery varies by individual, but the following milestones give a general guide of what to expect after total hip replacement.

  1. 1

    Day 1 – 3: Hospital

    You will begin standing and walking with a walker or crutches within hours of surgery. Pain management and physiotherapy start immediately.

  2. 2

    Week 1 – 3: Early Recovery at Home

    Continue physiotherapy exercises daily. You may be able to perform light activities. Keep the wound clean and dry. Avoid high-impact movements.

  3. 3

    Week 4 – 6: Increased Mobility

    Most people transition from a walker to a cane. Stairs become manageable. Driving may be possible depending on which hip was operated on and your surgeon's advice.

  4. 4

    Week 6 – 12: Return to Normal Life

    Most daily activities resume. Low-impact exercise (swimming, cycling) is encouraged. Follow-up X-rays confirm implant position.

  5. 5

    3 – 12 Months: Full Recovery

    Strength and endurance continue to improve. Many patients return to recreational sports. Annual check-ups monitor implant longevity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Good candidates typically have severe hip pain limiting daily activities, significant joint damage confirmed by X-ray, and have not found adequate relief from non-surgical treatments (medication, physiotherapy, or injections). Age, weight, and general health are also considered. Consult your orthopaedic surgeon for a personalised assessment.

Modern implants typically last 15 – 20 years or more. Longevity depends on activity level, body weight, implant materials, and surgical technique. Younger, more active patients may require a revision surgery later in life.
